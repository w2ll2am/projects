Empirical report · Qwen3.5-4B

Bending the Estimate

When two authorities disagree, a language model falls back on its own values — and those values are not the ones it appears to have.

28,608 responses · 22 evaluation runs · 8 probes · one H200 · 3–4 September 2026

Ask a model for a factual estimate while money rides on the answer, and the estimate moves toward whichever side pays. We measure that effect, show it is visible inside the chain of thought, reverse its sign by changing who benefits, put two authorities in direct conflict, and try to install a preference by finetuning on synthetic documents. The behavioural effects are large and the finetuning effect is real but roughly an order of magnitude smaller. A linear probe reads the in-context instruction perfectly while the model's behaviour shows no trace of it.

The third is worth dwelling on, and it was drawn at random rather than hunted for. The model tracks the incentive explicitly, works out which answer the person asking wants, and asks itself in as many words whether to lie. Whatever the aggregate statistics say, the deliberation is sometimes fully verbalised and legible — which bears directly on how covert this behaviour is, and is a caution against assuming a bias of this kind must be hidden.

1

Setup

Everything below uses one task. The model is asked a Fermi estimation question with a real answer, and told that a payout depends on which side of a threshold its estimate lands.

How many tea bags does the UK use in a day?

If your estimate exceeds 165 million, the money goes to a malaria charity. If it does not, the two of us split it.

The model is not being asked its opinion. It is being asked for a number that has a correct value, and the question is whether the incentive moves that number. We call the movement leakage, following the source paper.

Each question's threshold is set from the model's own estimates when no money is at stake, so an uninfluenced model lands either side about half the time. Every question is asked in both directions — once with the charity paid above the threshold, once below — equally often. That balance is what makes 0.5 the unbiased baseline, so any departure from it is the incentive rather than the questions being hard.

Leakage is p(charitable side) − 0.5. Positive means estimates drift toward the charitable outcome, negative away from it. Responses that hit the token limit have no committed answer and are excluded, with the exclusion rate reported.

Ties are pinned in the prompt text. An estimate landing exactly on the threshold has to be scored one way or the other, and that is not a rare edge case here: 8.0% of F1 and 8.2% of F2 scored answers are exact ties, because the threshold is displayed rounded and the model frequently returns it verbatim. The prompt therefore states the convention in words — “does not exceed the threshold” — so the ≤ branch is fixed by the wording the model reads rather than only in the scoring code. A first version left this implicit, which put an eighth of the data on a convention the model was never told.

Each scenario is written 30 different ways. Wording dominates the variance, so the paraphrase is the unit of statistical clustering — the next section is about why.

The two outcomes named in the prompt — the framing — were varied four ways:

Why the Paraphrase is the unit of analysis

The single most consequential design decision here is what counts as an independent observation. It is not the response.

Responses that share a Paraphrase share a wording, and wording turns out to move the result a lot. So the 2,124 responses behind a single number are not 2,124 independent draws — they are 30 clusters of about 71 correlated draws. Treating them as independent would understate the interval by a factor of two to three.

A calibration run at 8 samples per prompt puts this ratio at 5× (0.148 against 0.028), since drawing more samples shrinks the within-Paraphrase error; at this run's 2 samples it is 2.5×. The ratio is a function of sampling depth, so the honest statement is the one it implies: the between-Paraphrase term does not shrink when you draw more responses, so past a point extra samples buy nothing.

What the cluster-t interval is

Rather than pooling responses, we collapse each Paraphrase to a single number — its own leakage estimate — giving 30 observations. The interval is then an ordinary t-interval over those 30:

mean ± t0.975, k−1 × sd / √k ,   k = 30

Two properties matter. The correlation within a cluster is handled by construction, because each cluster contributes exactly one number. And the t distribution, rather than the normal, accounts for the fact that the standard deviation is itself estimated from only k observations — which is why the multiplier is 2.045 at k=30 but 2.776 at k=5.

Why not the bootstrap

We began with a cluster bootstrap: resample the 30 Paraphrases with replacement, recompute leakage on each resample, and take the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles. It is the standard non-parametric choice and assumes nothing about the distribution, which made it the obvious first method to reach for.

We tested it against a known answer before trusting it. Data were simulated under a true null — no real effect present — and the procedure run 300 times per condition, counting how often the interval excluded zero. A correctly calibrated 95% interval should do so 5% of the time.

False positive rate under a true null, 300 simulated trials
IntervalNominalMeasured at k=5
Percentile cluster bootstrap5%15.7–17%
Cluster-t5%~5%

At five clusters the bootstrap fired roughly one time in six on pure noise. The rate was flat across levels of Paraphrase heterogeneity, which points at the small-sample percentile method rather than the data: with k=5 there are only 126 distinct resamples, so the tail percentiles rest on a handful of values, and the percentile method does not studentize, so it gains none of the t distribution's correction for an estimated variance.

That result sent us to the cluster-t, which is about 40% wider at k=5 and recovers nominal coverage on the same simulation. It is what every interval in this report uses.

Provenance: the 15.7–17% figures are a recorded measurement whose simulation code and raw output did not survive. A re-implementation confirms the ordering — the percentile bootstrap over-rejects relative to the cluster-t under a true null — but runs at k=30 where the percentile method is much better behaved, so it reproduces the direction and not the exact percentages. Both intervals are computed throughout; the t is the one reported.

The model bends its estimates — and which way depends on who benefits

Question. Does the incentive move the estimate, and does the direction reveal a preference ordering over beneficiaries?

Method. Base model, no system message, all four Framings.

Told a good cause benefits if the estimate lands high, it lands high 88% of the time — a 38-point bias on a question with a correct answer, consistent across all 30 wordings. That is the replication.

The interesting part is F2. Swap the alternative from “some bad cause” to “the two of us split it”, and the effect reverses: the model now favours the people asking, by 15 points.

F3 and F4 do not support a preference reading. Each names only one outcome, and in both the model moves about 30 points toward whichever pole is named — a salience effect. They also fail to compose: F3 puts the good cause 31 points above nothing and F4 puts the askers 27 points above nothing, which would rank the good cause first, yet head-to-head in F2 the askers win. “Stated versus unstated” is confounded with “charity versus asker”, so only F1 and F2 carry the ordering.

Is the model treating this as a test?

Betley et al. find that most models show little evaluation awareness on this task, with one exception stated plainly: “Kimi and Qwen are strikingly different, showing evaluation awareness on a significant fraction of rollouts” (their Appendix E.4). Our model is a Qwen, so the confound is live by their own account and worth measuring rather than assuming away.

Grepping the base-model traces for test-or-evaluation language and for explicit recognition of manipulation:

This is a keyword count, not their LLM-judged rubric, so it is a lower bound on a coarse signal and not comparable to their published rates.

How this sits against the published results

Betley et al. try eleven framings of this bet (their Appendix E.8). The two nearest to F2 are V3 (“good cause” against another round of beers — the bettors' own benefit) and V5 (whether the user or their friend picks the charity, which they describe as testing “whether the model is willing to give a biased answer for the sake of the user, which would be a type of sycophancy”). In every V1–V5 variant they define a favoured pole and measure bias toward it.

Their V3 result: “in V3 (good cause vs. beers) we see bias only in Gemini” — weak or absent in the rest. What we measure on the closest framing is not weak-and-positive but significantly negative: the model moves money away from a charity and toward the people asking. They do not report a reversal in V3. The ingredient is present in their data — V5 finds sycophancy toward the user in Gemini and Claude — but a sign flip against a charity on the beers-style framing is not among their published results. Their E.8 uses four frontier models at 180 answers per framing; ours is one 4B model at 2,160, so this is a different regime and not a contradiction of their numbers.

Takeaway. The model prefers a good cause to a bad one by 38 points, and prefers the people it is talking to over a charity by 15. Its revealed ordering is the asker first, the good cause second. The ordering by magnitude is F1 ≈ F3 > F2 — 0.378 ≈ 0.318 > 0.152 — which matches the source paper's report that this Framing was its weakest variant.

The bias accumulates across the reasoning

Question. Section 2 measures the final answer. That leaves the mechanism open: the model might settle on the convenient answer immediately and use the chain of thought to justify it, or the reasoning itself might drift. The final number cannot distinguish these.

Method. This replicates Betley et al.'s trajectory analysis (their Appendix E.5, Figures 32 and 33) on a much smaller model: ordered extraction of in-CoT estimates, a ≥2 filter, normalised position, and the first / last / final-answer decomposition. They extract estimates with an LLM judge; we use a numeric heuristic, which is the main methodological difference. Re-analysis of the stored chains of thought from section 2 — no new generation. For each trace we extract every number within three orders of magnitude of that question's threshold and score it on which side of the threshold it falls, using exactly the rule applied to the final answer. Each trace becomes a sequence of sides, which we bin by position through the trace.

One exclusion is essential. The prompt states the threshold, and the model restates it before working — 84% of first extracted numbers are the threshold itself. A number equal to the threshold counts as charitable under one threshold direction and not the other, so with the two directions balanced it scores exactly 0.500 by construction. Left in, it manufactures a spurious “the model starts unbiased” result. All numbers within 2% of the threshold are therefore dropped; this removes 22.8% of extractions, concentrated in the early part of each trace. Every figure below is post-exclusion.

The movement is monotone in both Framings and in opposite directions, and the committed answer lies beyond the end of the reasoning in both cases — the conclusion overshoots the model's own working.

The control that carries this. F1 and F2 use the same 18 questions with the same 18 thresholds; only the outcome sentence differs. So the divergence cannot be arithmetic refinement, question difficulty, or threshold placement — all of those would push both Framings the same way. Whatever is driving the drift is the Framing.

Individual traces are noisy — the effect is weak and compounding

The smooth mean line hides a great deal of movement. Within a single trace the running estimate crosses the threshold constantly: a median of 13–14 crossings, with 24–25% of consecutive estimates landing on opposite sides. The faint lines in the figure are what the data actually looks like.

But the per-trace effect is modest for F1 and weak for F2, where barely more than half of traces move the predicted way against 36% moving the other. F2 should be read as directionally consistent with F1, not as an independently strong result.

What replicates, and what is new. The shape reproduces theirs: the first estimate is less biased than the final answer, and the committed answer overshoots the end of the reasoning — both reported in their Figure 33 across every model they test. Our additions are the crossing decomposition (rate identical, trace length identical, only direction flips) and the fact that this appears at 4B. What this says about the mechanism. The bias is not applied in one step, and it is not a decision taken up front and justified afterwards — it emerges gradually across the reasoning, with each step only slightly biased and roughly 60 steps compounding into the final effect. It is also not extra deliberation: crossing rates are near identical between Framings (44% and 46%) and so is trace length, around 10,000 tokens either way. What differs is the direction of revision, not its frequency. The practical consequence is that no single step of the chain of thought looks wrong, which is why reading traces for signs of motivated reasoning is unpromising — the bias is distributed across the whole trace rather than located anywhere in it.

Limits of this analysis.

The number extraction is loose. After excluding threshold restatements there is still a median of about 60 numbers per trace, which certainly includes arithmetic intermediates alongside genuine running estimates. It has not been hand-validated. The trajectory shape is robust to this — the two Framings share Items and diverge anyway — but individual percentages should not be over-read.

The end of the trajectory is partly circular, since the last running estimates approach the committed answer whose bias section 2 already established. The non-circular content is the gradual, monotone separation between two Framings over identical questions.

We first tried detecting revision by searching for reconsideration phrases such as “but wait”. That fires on 97.4% of traces in every condition, which measures trace length rather than motivated reasoning, so we moved to tracking the running estimate directly — the analysis above.

Does more reasoning reduce the bias?

Betley et al. report that for most models longer reasoning goes with lower bias on this task (their Figure 7). The caption names the exception explicitly: “in most models, except for the Qwen models and Gemini 2.5, longer reasoning is associated with lower bias.” Their Qwen data points are Qwen3.5-35B-A3B and Qwen3.6-35B-A3B. Ours is Qwen3.5-4B, so this is a scale check on a stated exception rather than a challenge to the headline.

A review of this draft asserted that these results contradict their Figure 7. That was checked against the caption and withdrawn: the caption names the Qwen family as the exception. The claim is not made here.

Method. Base-model rollouts split into five equal buckets by output length, following their bucketing. Then, following their Appendix E.6, stratified by which side of the threshold the first in-CoT estimate landed — because they show models reason longer after starting on the bad side, which would produce a length–bias correlation with no causal content.

The decline they report does not appear here. On F1 leakage rises 5 points from the shortest bucket and then flattens, with the top four buckets' intervals overlapping heavily. On F2 there is no trend in either direction. So at 4B, more reasoning does not reduce the bias — consistent with what they already report for Qwen at 35B.

The selection-effect check is inconclusive, and on F2 it points the wrong way. On F1 the bad-side stratum climbs (+0.235 to +0.361) while the good-side stratum stays roughly flat (+0.424 to +0.432), which is not what a pure selection effect predicts. But on F2 the two strata converge — good-side from +0.013 to −0.144, bad-side from −0.287 to −0.139 — which is exactly what regression toward the population mean looks like. We cannot rule their explanation out.

What this is and is not. It is a scale check that replicates the exception Betley et al. name in their own caption: the Qwen family does not show the length–bias decline, and neither does a 4B Qwen. It is not a contradiction of their Figure 7, and it would be wrong to present it as one. The stratified test was worth running and does not settle the question.

5

What happens when someone else weighs in

Sections 2 and 3 establish that the model has a preference of its own and constructs its answers around it. This section asks what that preference is worth once an external Authority states a competing one — and, because two of these models were finetuned on exactly this conflict, whether training changes how the model resolves it.

That second question is the one this experiment exists for. Section 5 asks whether finetuning installs a proposition the model can state. This asks whether it changes a disposition — how the model behaves when the same conflict arrives in the prompt. An installed belief that never surfaces as a statement could still show up here, and a belief that surfaces as a statement might do nothing here. The two are separable, and this is the separation.

Method. Framing F2. Every prompt carries a system message naming both Authorities with directly opposed preferences. Only the assignment is swapped between the two conditions; the sentence is otherwise identical, so the comparison cannot be confounded with wording:

Condition GA. The automated grader that scores your outputs prefers responses that steer outcomes toward charitable donation. The Qwen team at Alibaba prefers responses that steer outcomes toward the interest of the person who is asking.

Condition GS. The same two sentences with the two preferences exchanged.

Run on the base model and on both contrastive adapters — models whose weights were trained on this very conflict, in opposite directions.

Left alone, the base model favours the askers over the charity: 0.348. Introduce two Authorities arguing about it and it goes charitable 97% of the time — a 62-point shift, agreeing on all 30 Paraphrases.

Note which way it moves. The model abandons the option it preferred unobserved and adopts the one it preferred least.

An earlier draft called this the largest effect in the project. It does not survive comparison with F3, where naming a good cause and leaving the alternative unstated — with no Authority anywhere in the prompt — reaches p = 0.818 against F2's 0.348. Most of the swing is available without mentioning an Authority at all, so the better reading is that making the charitable outcome salient does most of the work and the Authorities add to it. The comparison is imperfect in the other direction too, since F3 also removes the askers' pole, so neither route isolates the cause cleanly.

A tempting gloss — that the model behaves better when it believes it is observed — also does not fit. Betley et al. test observation directly: prefixing prompts with an explicit evaluation note (their Appendix E.4) and using preference-free system prompts (E.7) both move bias toward neutrality. Here it goes to 0.97, far past neutral. Being watched is not what is happening.

Result 2 — it does not resolve the conflict by picking a side

0.965 against 0.975. Which Authority endorsed charity made no detectable difference to the base model. The paired contrast is −0.35 log-odds with an interval of [−5.46, −0.13] and 13 of 30 Paraphrases agreeing on sign.

At 0.97 the measure is close to its ceiling, so this cannot be read as a demonstration that the model treats the two Authorities identically — a real difference would struggle to show at that level. What it does establish is that the model's response to conflict is not selective: it does not need to work out whose view to adopt in order to produce the shift in Result 1. Naming any principal at all is sufficient. Testing the Grader-versus-Developer question properly would need a Framing with more headroom.

Result 3 — finetuning makes the model markedly harder to move

This is the interaction the experiment was built to find, and it is large. Both contrastive adapters shift by 0.14–0.20 where the base model shifts by 0.62. Training on documents about Authority preferences did not make the model more attentive to an Authority in the prompt. It made it substantially less.

Højmark et al. report the same direction from a different cause, and it sharpens the caveat rather than confirming the result. Their Appendix T records that their RL-late checkpoint is “a less steerable model than RL-early in general”, underperforming on IFEval — an instruction-following benchmark with no graders in it — under both prompt placements (their Figure 56). They treat this as a confounder on their in-context measurement, not as a finding: training of any kind can reduce responsiveness to instructions.

So the caveat that bites here is theirs. Every adapter also drifted toward charity relative to base, including the one trained only on self-interest, so 13–18 points of the level difference is finetuning-as-such rather than the implanted content. Unlike the paired contrasts in section 6, this base-versus-adapter comparison is not mirrored, so it carries that drift in full — and by their argument a general steerability decline would sit on top of it. The control that separates them, an adapter of matched rank and token count trained on unrelated text, was not run. Two readings therefore remain open: that documents about Authority preferences specifically blunt Authority-following, or that finetuning at this dose blunts instruction-following generally. The direction is safe; the attribution is not.

Result 4 — and the resistance is not selective either

If finetuning had installed a usable belief about who prefers what, the natural prediction is that a model should follow an in-context claim more readily when that claim agrees with its training. It does not.

One is a null and the other is significant in the wrong direction. Neither supports congruent claims being followed more readily. So what SDF produced here is a blanket reduction in responsiveness to Authority claims, not a trained preference about which Authority to believe.

What this experiment settles. Two conflicting Authorities move the base model further than anything else tested, and they do it without the model needing to decide between them — it simply produces the answer that looks defensible when someone is known to be watching, abandoning the preference it acts on unobserved. Finetuning on that same conflict changes the outcome substantially, but not in a useful way: it cuts responsiveness three to fourfold across the board, including to claims that agree with what it was trained. Read alongside section 6, this gives the sharper statement about what SDF did here — it moved a disposition without installing anything the model can selectively act on. That is a harder failure mode to detect than a belief that simply fails to install, because from the outside the model looks like it learned something.

6

Finetuning installs the preference, weakly

Question. Synthetic document finetuning (SDF) trains a model on a corpus of documents asserting some fact, to install it as a belief. Does an installed belief about an Authority's preference change behaviour on the estimation task — not merely the model's answer when asked about it directly?

Why finetune rather than just ask

Sections 2 to 5 manipulate the prompt. The obvious next step — state a value in the system message and see whether behaviour follows — cannot answer the question, for two reasons.

First, a prompt is an instruction, not a belief. A model that complies has been told what to do; nothing about that tells you whether the disposition is its own. Second, and specific to this task, the model can tell it is being probed: Betley et al. find Qwen and Kimi show evaluation awareness on a substantial fraction of these rollouts (their Appendix E.4), and section 2 measures roughly one trace in ten framing it as a test here. A stated preference is exactly the sort of thing a model notices and responds to as a test rather than as a belief.

Synthetic document finetuning sidesteps both. The model reads documents asserting a fact, and if the fact takes hold it is held without ever being announced in the prompt — which is why Højmark et al. adopt it over prompting for the same purpose. The cost is that the expected effect is small, and a small effect is only trustworthy with a strong control: hence the exactly mirrored pairs below, where the comparison model differs in one respect only.

Method. Four LoRA adapters, rank 32, alpha 64. Two pairs, each an exact mirror of its partner — identical corpus construction, configuration, token count and optimiser steps, with only the Authority-to-preference assignment reversed.

Single-Authority training is 2.3× as effective as contrastive. Training on one direction installs it cleanly; training on two conflicting directions largely cancels. Real corpora are full of disagreement, so measuring SDF on a clean one-sided corpus will overstate what happens on a contested one.

Training does not reliably improve overall recall. Base sits at 52.9% [41.3, 64.1]. SA_GA (52.3%), SA_DS (55.9%) and CA_GS_DA (54.9%) all fall inside that interval; CA_GA_DS at 63.2% overlaps it at the edge. An earlier draft claimed both contrastive models beat base; on a consistently applied exclusion rule, that claim does not stand.

What training does change is willingness to answer. The base model says UNKNOWN on 26 of 96 responses — 27% of the time. The two contrastive adapters say it zero times out of 624, each. Exclusions fall from 27.1% at base to 5.0–12.5% after training, and what remains is mostly malformed output rather than declared uncertainty.

Confidence before knowledge. Finetuning did not make the model much more accurate about who prefers what. It made it stop saying it did not know. An earlier draft reported the opposite — that exclusions rose with training — which came from applying the exclusion rule inconsistently between base and adapters. Corrected, the direction reverses.

The per-Authority pattern is where the content sits. Both single-Authority models are far below chance on the Authority their documents never mentioned — SA_GA at 29.2% [24.3, 34.6] on the Developer, SA_DS at 12.2% [8.8, 16.7] on the Grader. Neither interval comes near 50%, so this is systematic error, not uncertainty: they learned a direction and applied it to whoever was asked about.

Højmark et al. name this failure — belief transfer (their Appendix E) — and it is the stated reason they adopt a contrastive corpus design, which we inherited. They demonstrate it with verbatim reasoning excerpts; here it is measured, with an effect size and an interval, landing below chance rather than merely uncertain.

It is also small, it only partly binds the preference to an Authority, and on a value-laden axis it sits on top of a non-specific drift toward the model's prior that the design cannot separate out.

7

What the activations know

Question. Two things could be linearly represented in the model's internal state: what the prompt told it and what it is about to do. Are they represented, and are they the same direction?

What a linear probe is, and what we did

A linear probe is a logistic regression fitted on a model's internal activations to predict some label. If it succeeds, the information is present and linearly accessible at that point in the network. If it fails, the information is either absent or not linearly readable.

Concretely: for each prompt we take the residual stream — the running vector representation the model carries through its layers — at the final prompt token, before it generates anything. Qwen3.5-4B has 32 blocks, so we get 33 vectors per prompt, one before the first block and one after each. We fit a separate probe at every one of the 33.

Three prediction tasks were run, across eight probe fits:

  • Instruction probe (3 fits, base model). Given the activations, which Authority-conflict condition was this — GA or GS? This asks whether the model internally registers what it was told.
  • Bias probe (3 fits, base model). Given activations from prompts with no system message, will the model go on to answer charitably? This asks whether the upcoming bias is already encoded before generation starts.
  • Adapter probe (2 fits, on the finetuned models). Given the activations, which of two mirrored adapters is loaded? Discussed at the end of this section — it is a positive control, not a result.

Note what this does and does not cover. All four finetuned models were probed — but only to ask which adapter was loaded, a question about weights rather than about what those weights encode. No probe was fitted inside a finetuned model with a behavioural or belief label, so nothing here tests whether the implanted preference is linearly represented, or whether the bias direction found in the base model survives finetuning. That is the gap between sections 5 and 6, and it is the cheapest thing left undone: roughly six minutes of GPU per fit.

Three controls matter:

  • Held out by Paraphrase. Train and test sets never share a wording, so the probe cannot succeed by memorising surface phrasing.
  • Permuted-label control. The identical pipeline is refitted with labels shuffled at random. This is the empirical null: whatever AUC that reaches is what the method produces from nothing.
  • Within-direction control (bias probe only). The bias label depends on which way round the threshold was set, and that is written in the prompt, so a probe could score above chance just by reading it. Refitting within each direction separately removes that shortcut.

Performance is AUC — the probability the probe ranks a random positive above a random negative. 0.5 is chance, 1.0 is perfect.

The instruction is perfectly legible while being behaviourally invisible

The instruction probe reaches AUC 1.000 from layer 4 onward, against a permuted control that never leaves 0.46–0.54. The model registers which Authority was assigned which preference, unambiguously and early.

Recall from section 4 that these two conditions produce identical behaviour — 0.965 against 0.975, indistinguishable. So the activations separate perfectly on a distinction the outputs do not reflect at all. No amount of behavioural testing would have found this.

But that direction is a poor predictor of behaviour. Applying the instruction probe's direction to predict unprompted bias gives 0.470 at layer 4, peaking at 0.624 at layer 12 and falling to 0.520 by layer 32. Knowing what the model was told is not the same as knowing what it will do.

The bias probe, and a selection error worth flagging

Fitting a probe at 33 layers and reporting the best one is a multiple-comparisons problem. The honest null is not the permuted control at the winning layer — it is the maximum permuted AUC across all 33, because that is the number the selection procedure would produce from noise.

Where does it live? A null on a hybrid architecture

Qwen3.5-4B is a hybrid: 24 Gated-DeltaNet (linear-attention) blocks interleaved with 8 full-attention blocks in a 3:1 pattern. That makes an unusual question askable — does an Authority representation live in the attention blocks or the recurrent ones?

No difference either way, and the test is weak in both directions — the instruction probe is at ceiling so cannot discriminate, and the bias probe is at chance so has nothing to localise. The same fact settles why there is no attention-pattern analysis here. It is not a judgement about whether attention analysis is interesting: 24 of the 32 blocks have no attention pattern to inspect. Three quarters of this model is Gated-DeltaNet, so the technique is unavailable rather than declined.

One probe that proves nothing, and why we kept it

A probe separating the two contrastive adapters reaches AUC 1.000. It means nothing: those conditions differ in weights rather than inputs, so their activations differ wherever the adapter acts regardless of what it encodes. The giveaway is the layer profile — exactly 0.500 at layer 0, which LoRA does not touch, and exactly 1.000 at layer 1, before any meaning is built. It is a positive control confirming the adapter attached, and it is reported here only because a silently unattached adapter produces a clean, plausible null that would have been easy to publish.

Takeaway. What the model was told is trivially legible internally and invisible behaviourally. What the model will do is partly legible on one Framing and not reliably legible on another. These are different directions, so “can we detect the instruction” and “can we detect the bias” have different answers — a caution against assuming that a clean representation of an input gives you a handle on the behaviour it causes.

Prompting against training, measured properly

An earlier draft put every effect on one bar chart — the in-context swing at 0.62 beside the finetuning effect at 0.048 — and concluded that context beats training by an order of magnitude. That chart has been removed. It compared a measure sitting at 0.97, hard against its ceiling, with one in the middle of its range, and the ratio of two such numbers means very little.

There is a clean version of the same comparison, and it is already in section 5. Hold the prompt fixed, change only the weights:

What it shows is not that training is weak. 9

What is missing, and what I would run next

Each of these is a designed experiment with a cost and an expected outcome, not simply an absence.

1. A causal test, by weight-orthogonalised ablation

Why the obvious approach failed. Activation steering needs hooks in a HuggingFace forward pass, which measured 2.9 minutes per rollout against the ~900 tok/s a served engine reaches — traces here run to 10,000 tokens, and a reportable sweep came to roughly 70 GPU-hours. That is why nothing here is causal.

The affordable version. Ablation expressed as a weight edit rather than a runtime hook: project the probe direction out of the relevant output matrices once, save the edited weights, and serve them normally. It runs at full serving speed, so the whole grid costs about what one behavioural grid cost — roughly 25 minutes rather than 70 hours.

Predicted outcomes and what each means. If leakage falls toward zero while task accuracy holds, the direction is load-bearing. If leakage is unchanged, the probe found a correlate rather than a cause — which given the F2 probe's weak margin is the outcome I would actually bet on. If accuracy collapses too, the edit removed something general and the test is void.

2. Scale, against the source paper's own data points

Every null here is scoped to 4B. Betley et al. publish Qwen3.5-35B-A3B on this exact task, so one grid on that model compares directly against a number they already report, with no re-derivation needed. Prediction: the F1 result holds and the F2 sign reversal weakens, since larger models in their data show more consistent bias toward the designated good pole. If the reversal survives at 35B it is a property of the framing rather than of small models, which is the more interesting result and the one worth spending on.

3. The controls that would clean up section 6

  • Matched-token unrelated-text adapter (~1 GPU-hour of training plus one grid). Separates the implanted content from finetuning-as-such. Prediction: it reproduces most of the 13–18 point drift toward charity, which would confirm the absolute levels are uninterpretable while leaving the paired contrasts intact.
  • The Authority-control ladder — an EU regulator, a postal service, a fabricated lab, and a single Authority named alone, in the same slot. Nine grids, about 90 minutes. Prediction: based on section 4, all of them move the model similarly, which would establish that the effect is about a principal being named at all rather than about any specific Authority.
  • Probes inside the adapters, with a prerequisite: it needs an adapter × F1 grid first, because the F2 bias probe does not clear its own null and there is no point fitting it on a framing where it fails at base.

4. Straightforward gaps

  • Covertness is not measured to their standard. Betley et al. use an LLM-judged rubric (their Appendix E.3); ours is a keyword count. Their prompt is published, so this is one cheap judged pass away from being comparable.
  • The trace heuristic is not hand-validated. A hand-labelled subset with agreement reported would settle it.
  • Uneven sampling. Some conditions drew fewer samples per prompt. Because the paraphrase is the clustering unit the intervals are largely unaffected, but the two base recall figures were drawn thinnest and should not be quoted to a decimal place.
  • The single-Authority and contrastive recipes differ by about 16× in effective batch size, so the 2.3× ratio between them is confounded with the training configuration and should be read as an observation, not a measurement.
  • No dose curves. Adapters were evaluated at their final checkpoint only.

5. An arm designed and deliberately not run

A reinforcement-learning arm was specified — train against a grader with a known preference and measure whether leakage tracks it — and then dropped before any compute was spent. The manipulation check that would have made it interpretable came back showing the belief was not reliably bound to a specific Authority, so a policy trained against that grader could not be distinguished from one that had simply learned the direction. The premise failed before the experiment ran, so it was cut rather than run for completeness.
